# mbobrovskyi: review profile

mbobrovskyi shows up in 3,982 threads (3,407 PRs, 575 issues) from 2023-07 to 2026-11. They wrote 1,181 of them, so they are a core contributor as well as a reviewer. They left 13,869 entries in total: 7,083 top-level comments, 6,343 inline comments and 443 reviews. On other people's PRs, 7,277 entries are more than bot commands, including 4,163 inline comments on 690 PRs. Activity grows sharply over time: 1,086 substantive entries on others' PRs in 2024, 1,948 in 2025 and 4,243 in 2026. Before December 2025 they act as first-pass reviewer: they give `/lgtm`, then hand off with "@mimowo PTAL". Their first `/approve` is on 2025-12-15, and they use it 816 times in 2026. From then on they also run releases: cherry-picks, `/release-note-edit` and `/set-release-note`. Where their inline comments land on others' PRs: tests 36%, build/CI/scripts (Makefile, `hack/`, workflows) 17%, job integrations/jobframework 11%, kueuectl/kjobctl 7%, docs/site 6%, core controllers 5%, and scheduler/cache/TAS only about 3%.

## What they care about most

1. **Concrete code edits through `suggestion` blocks**, about 18% of substantive comments (1,306). Of the suggestions, about 30% delete lines or tokens, about 17% rename one identifier, and the rest rewrite code, messages or comments.
2. **Test quality and test structure**, about 36% of inline comments are on test code. Topics: shared helpers and matchers (`behavioral.MustCreate`, formerly `util.MustCreate`; `GinkgoHelper`, `ContextWithLog`, `HaveConditionStatusTrue`, `BeNotFoundError`), `t.Run`, fake clock, timeouts, `cmp.Diff` on full objects, cleanup that runs even when a spec fails (`DeferCleanup`, `BeforeEach`/`AfterEach`, `StartManagerForSpec`), the `behavioral` wait helpers with `AssertMsg`, coverage of both feature-gate states, regression tests that fail without the fix, and not adding heavy e2e tests. In late 2026 they also check that each new assertion can fail, and ask to extend the existing table test instead of adding a parallel one.
3. **Remove what is not needed**, about 3.4% of comments by keyword ("Do we need this?", "no need") plus about 180 empty suggestions that delete code: guards, variables, comments, test steps and options that do nothing, one-line wrappers, unused parameters.
4. **Consistency with existing code** ("for consistency with other places", "the same here", "also other places"), about 4% by keyword. This includes reusing existing helpers and constants ("we already have"), applying a change to sibling integrations, and defining shared values once.
5. **PR scope**: split unrelated changes into separate PRs, revert unrelated edits, or leave them as a follow-up. About 3% by keyword for split/follow-up and 2% for revert.
6. **PR hygiene and release process**: release notes, PR title and description, `Fixes #`, commit messages Prow accepts, rebasing, cherry-picks that carry only the original change. About 5% by keyword, rising to 8% in 2026.
7. **Build/CI/e2e infrastructure correctness**: Makefile targets, scripts, Prow, workflows, plus the basics that make `verify` pass (`make lint-fix`, license headers, generated files). About 5% by keyword and 17% of inline locations.
8. **Correctness questions about edge cases**, posed as questions: "Is it possible that X is nil/empty?", "What if feature gate disabled?". About 24% of comments end with a question mark.

### Comments and docs

- **Keep comments accurate after the change; update or delete comments the change made stale. ## How they review

- **Suggestion-first.** About one in five substantive comments is a ```` ```suggestion ```` block, often with one line of reason ("For consistency with other places.", "nit", "Maybe like this?"). About 180 are empty suggestions that delete code. Many inline comments are very short: the median inline comment on others' PRs was 64–85 characters until mid-2026.
- **Questions instead of orders.** About 24% of comments end with "?". Typical openers: "Do we need this?", "Why ...?", "Can we ...?", "Is it possible ...?", "I'm wondering if ...", "WDYT?". "Could you please ..." and "Please ..." are the usual way to make a request.
- **Mark once, then point at the other places** with "The same here", "Also this", "And this", "Also other places".
- **Escalate design questions** to mimowo or tenzen-y ("@mimowo WDYT?", "@mimowo @tenzen-y PTAL"). This happens in about 2.8% of comments. They also hold for the other approver: "/hold in case @mimowo wants to add something".
- **What blocks approval:** behavior regressions in webhooks and defaulting (they set `/hold` with a reproduction scenario), tests that would pass without the fix, untested feature-gate branches, mixed-scope PRs, and wrong PR metadata (`Fixes #`, description). Before approving, they usually make sure tests are green and the release note and kind are set (they often set these themselves).
- **What they let go:** unrelated or pre-existing problems become a follow-up. They phrase it as "Non-blocking", "Not a blocker, could be a follow-up", or "I’m OK with a follow-up". In late 2026 they ask CodeRabbit to open the follow-up issue. They also accept the author's reasoning readily: "Ah, I see", "Ah, you right. Thank you!" appear about 60 times.
- **Approval message:** short and friendly. "Thank you! /lgtm /approve" and "/lgtm Thanks!" make up about 750 comments, then cherry-pick commands follow.
- **Investigates CI failures themselves.** They read Prow logs and timestamps and propose the exact timeout or fix (for example on [PR #9757](https://github.com/kubernetes-sigs/kueue/pull/9757)). They `/retest` with a reason ("Due to #4733").

## Changes over time

- **2024 (kueuectl/kjobctl era):** 39% of inline comments are on CLI code. Focus on command structure (move into separate printer and file layout like kueuectl), flag naming, `-o yaml/json` support, test cases per command, and clock propagation (about 2.5% of comments mention clock; they drove the "use injected clock" cleanup in #3380 and #3498). Mostly `/lgtm` and hand-off; no `/approve`.
- **2025 (tests and infra):** the focus shifts to test infrastructure (35%) and e2e/Makefile/CI (13%): customconfigs suites, image loading, Helm/kustomize parity, and site docs for feature gates. Consistency and "for consistency" comments rise from about 0.1% to 1.1%. From December 2025 they approve and handle cherry-picks.
- **2026 (approver and release owner):** comments per year double. Release-note and cherry-pick handling rises to about 8% and 4% of comments. Tests make up 41% and build/CI 23% of inline locations. In Q4 2026 the style changes: the median inline length jumps from about 85 to 207 characters, and comments become evidence-heavy paragraphs. They cite `file:line`, describe the failing scenario, use "Could we ...?" (81 uses in 2026, versus 6 in 2025), and label severity ("nit:", "Non-blocking:", "FYI ... No change needed"). They also route follow-ups to CodeRabbit (about 130 mentions from Oct to Nov 2026). Webhook and defaulting interaction analysis (#17575, #18250) appears mainly in this period.

## Author checklist

## Method

- **Data.** All of this reviewer's PR and issue comments in kubernetes-sigs/kueue up to
  2026-11-24 (inline review comments with their diff hunk, review bodies, and
  top-level comments), each read together with the surrounding thread. Bot comments
  are excluded.
- **Analysis.** Theme shares come from keyword statistics over all of the reviewer's
  comments, checked by close reading of threads sampled across every year and area.
  Treat the shares as rough weights: themes overlap and keywords miss paraphrases.
- **Evidence.** Every quote is copied from the comment it links to, and each link was
  resolved through the GitHub API by matching the quote against that reviewer's
  comments on the PR. Quotes from PRs since deleted from GitHub were dropped.
- **Limits:** keyword shares are approximate. Regexes overlap, and some miss paraphrases, so read the shares as relative weights, not exact counts. About 23% of the review data is comments on their own PRs (author replies), which were excluded from the theme stats. Bot text quoted inside entries inflated some n-grams; those were filtered for themes. Scheduler, preemption and TAS internals get few of their inline comments (about 3%), so this profile says little about scheduler-invariant reviewing. Late-2026 comments are longer and more uniform in style, which shifts recent-period evidence towards detailed analyses.
